## 1. Parties & roles

This Data Processing Agreement ("**DPA**") is entered into between:

- **"Processor"**: Force Media (operating as **Force AI**), a Kuwait-based provider of
  the Force Website Builder ("**FWB**") multi-tenant e-commerce storefront platform.
- **"Controller"**: the merchant identified in the FWB order form / account signup
  ("**Merchant**"), who operates a storefront on FWB and determines the purposes and
  means of processing its customers' ("**Shoppers**'") personal data.

This DPA supplements and forms part of the FWB Terms of Service / merchant agreement
between the parties (the "**Principal Agreement**"). In the event of a conflict between
this DPA and the Principal Agreement regarding the processing of personal data, this DPA
prevails.

The Merchant is the **data controller** for Shopper personal data collected through its
storefront (it decides what to sell, what data to collect at checkout, and what
marketing to run). Force AI is the **data processor**, processing that data solely to
provide the FWB platform to the Merchant.

**This DPA applies GCC-wide.** FWB serves merchants across Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, the
UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, and Oman. The **data protection law that governs a given Shopper's
personal data is the law of the Merchant's own jurisdiction** — i.e. the country where
the Merchant is established and where its storefront principally operates — not
automatically Kuwaiti law merely because Force AI is Kuwait-based. §6 and Annex 4 set out
the governing data-protection-law analysis and cross-border-transfer basis per country.
(This is separate from §8's governing-law-of-_this-contract_ clause, which is a
commercial choice, not a statement about which data-protection statute applies to a
Shopper's data.)

## 2. Subject matter, nature, purpose, and duration of processing

- **Subject matter:** provision of the FWB multi-tenant e-commerce platform (storefront,
  checkout, order management, customer accounts, push/email notifications, product
  analytics) to the Merchant.
- **Nature of processing:** collection, storage, retrieval, transmission, and erasure of
  Shopper personal data as necessary to operate an online store — account creation,
  order placement and fulfillment, payment initiation, shipping, customer support,
  loyalty/wishlist features, and (where the Shopper consents) marketing/analytics.
- **Purpose:** to enable the Merchant to sell products/services to Shoppers and manage
  the resulting customer relationship, order lifecycle, and store operations.
- **Duration:** for as long as the Merchant maintains an active FWB store, plus the
  retention periods described in Annex 1 and `docs/RETENTION.md`, plus the backup
  persistence window described in §9 (Deletion / return of data) and Annex 2.

## 3. Categories of data subjects and personal data

**Data subjects:** the Merchant's Shoppers (customers/visitors of the storefront), and,
incidentally, the Merchant's own staff/admin users of the FWB admin panel.

**Categories of personal data** (see Annex 1 for the full table, derived from
`docs/privacy/data-map.md`):

- Identity data: first/last name, email, phone, date of birth (optional)
- Account credentials: password hash (never plaintext), MFA status
- Address data: shipping/billing addresses
- Order data: order history, line items, order notes, payment status/reference
  (**not** raw card numbers — see §10)
- Behavioral/device data: wishlist, cart, abandoned-cart, loyalty points, reviews,
  returns, push-notification subscription identifiers
- Technical/security data: IP address, user agent, in security audit logs only
- Marketing data (consent-gated only): analytics events, ad-pixel events

FWB does **not** knowingly process special categories of data (health, biometric,
religious, political, etc.) as a platform default; a Merchant that collects such data
through custom fields does so outside FWB's designed data model and is solely
responsible for the applicable legal basis.

## 9. Deletion, return, and the honest backup-persistence boundary

This section states plainly, for the Merchant's benefit, what "deletion" actually means
on FWB — taken directly from `docs/privacy/data-map.md` §5:

- **Live systems**: a Shopper-initiated erasure (`POST /auth/me/erase`, §10) or a
  Merchant-initiated account deletion updates FWB's live production database and
  triggers third-party propagation (§10) within minutes, best-effort.
- **Neon Point-in-Time-Recovery window: 7 days.** If production is ever restored from a
  backup point predating an erasure, that Shopper's pre-erasure data can reappear until
  the erasure is re-applied (erasure is idempotent and safe to re-run).
- **Daily encrypted database dumps (local + Cloudflare R2): 30-day retention on both
  layers.** An erased Shopper's data persists in progressively older dumps until they
  roll off this window — the **longest** possible reappearance window, up to 30 days.
- **Audit logs are the one intentional exception**: security/audit-trail rows
  (`audit_logs`) are retained 90 days hot, then archived to R2 for 7 years, and are
  **not** individually erasable on Shopper request — this is a deliberate design choice
  (tamper-evident record of who-did-what; GDPR Art. 17(3)(b) recognises a legal-obligation
  carve-out for exactly this class of record) and is disclosed here rather than left
  silent.

**Plain-language summary for the Merchant:** "deleted" means immediately gone from the
live platform and best-effort removed from configured third parties within minutes; a
genuinely complete purge across every backup copy takes up to 30 days.

## 10. Data subject rights — what FWB already implements

FWB ships two self-service endpoints under the Shopper's own authenticated session (no
Merchant or Force AI staff intervention required for a Shopper to exercise these):

- **`GET /auth/me/export`** (`apps/api/src/routes/auth.routes.ts`, backed by
  `AuthService.exportAccount`) — right to data portability. Returns a JSON bundle of
  every row across the schema tied to that Shopper: profile, addresses, orders (with
  line items and payments), abandoned carts, wishlists, reviews, returns, loyalty
  points/transactions, and push-notification subscriptions. Served as a downloadable
  attachment. Logged to the audit trail.
- **`POST /auth/me/erase`** (backed by `AuthService.eraseAccount`) — irreversible
  anonymization, not merely a soft-delete. In a single database transaction it: hard-
  deletes pure-PII rows (addresses, carts, wishlists, push subscriptions, refresh
  tokens, loyalty records); nulls the customer link on orders and reviews while
  preserving order totals/line-items/order-number (**Kuwait Ministry of Finance ≥5-year
  invoice-retention requirement**) and scrubbing the address JSON snapshot; and rewrites
  the user row itself (randomised email/password, name set to "Erased User", DOB/avatar
  cleared, account marked inactive). It then fires a best-effort, asynchronous
  third-party propagation event (OneSignal push-subscription deletion, PostHog person
  deletion where configured) that cannot roll back the primary anonymization if it
  fails. Idempotent — safe to call twice. **Limit:** cannot be invoked by a Merchant/
  platform-admin account (`role !== 'customer'` is rejected) — it is Shopper-initiated
  only; a Merchant-initiated erasure on a Shopper's behalf currently requires the
  Merchant to ask the Shopper to log in and self-serve, or requires a manual
  Force AI-operated path (**not currently self-service for the Merchant — see Known
  gaps**).
- Separately, **`DELETE /auth/me`** is a _soft_-delete (`deletedAt` timestamp) that
  deactivates an account without erasing PII — distinct from, and not a substitute for,
  the erase endpoint above.

Both endpoints are logged to the audit trail (`user.data.exported` /
`user.account.erased`) with the acting user, IP, and user agent.

## 11. Security measures — summary (full detail in Annex 2)

Only measures **confirmed present in the codebase** are described below and in Annex 2;
anything not confirmed is called out in "Known gaps," not asserted here.

- **Encryption in transit:** TLS on every public endpoint (Cloudflare-fronted apps +
  API); HSTS enforced at the API (`max-age=31536000; includeSubDomains; preload`,
  `apps/api/src/middleware/security.ts`).
- **Encryption at rest:** Neon Postgres (AES-256, `fwb-eu` project, `aws-eu-central-1`
  Frankfurt region); Cloudflare R2 (encrypted at rest by default). **Sensitive
  credentials at the application layer** (payment-provider API keys, shipping-carrier
  credentials, WhatsApp-bridge tokens, MFA secrets, marketing-pixel secrets, AI-agent
  keys) are additionally encrypted with **AES-256-GCM + PBKDF2 key derivation** before
  they ever reach the database (`apps/api/src/services/encryption.service.ts`, used
  across 18 call sites in the API).
- **Authentication:** rotating refresh-token model (30-minute access JWT with `jti`
  denylist + bulk-revocation version claim; 365-day DB-backed refresh token with
  reuse-detection → family revocation on replay); MFA supported on admin accounts.
- **Tenant isolation:** Postgres row-level security (`FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY`) as an
  independent layer below API-level role/store-ownership checks — the application's own
  DB connection cannot read cross-tenant rows outside an explicit, audited bypass
  helper. Extensive RLS migration history (10+ migrations closing coverage gaps).
- **Audit logging:** security-relevant actions logged to `audit_logs` (actor, action,
  resource, IP, user agent), 90 days hot in Postgres then archived to Cloudflare R2 for
  7 years (tamper-evident record).
- **Backups:** daily `pg_dump` to local storage **and** to Cloudflare R2, client-side
  encrypted before upload, 30-day rolling retention on both layers; Neon native
  point-in-time recovery, 7-day window. Both restore paths have been drilled (not just
  configured) — see `docs/DB-BACKUP-RESTORE.md`.
- **CI/CD security gates:** dependency audit (`npm audit`, hard-gated on high severity),
  secret scanning (gitleaks), static analysis (semgrep), and nightly OWASP ZAP DAST
  against the production storefront — all on every PR/push per
  `docs/security/security-overview.md`.

## Annex 2 — Technical and organisational measures

_(Only measures confirmed present in the codebase as of 2026-09-22 — see §11 for the citations backing each line; nothing aspirational.)_

1. TLS in transit on all public endpoints; HSTS at the API.
2. AES-256 encryption at rest for the primary database (Neon) and object storage (R2).
3. Application-layer AES-256-GCM + PBKDF2 envelope encryption for stored third-party
   credentials (payment, shipping, messaging, MFA, AI-agent keys).
4. Row-level security enforced at the database layer, independent of and in addition to
   API-level authorization checks.
5. Rotating, reuse-detected refresh tokens; short-lived (30 min) access tokens;
   MFA available on admin accounts.
6. Role-based access control (super_admin / store_admin / staff / customer) with
   per-store ownership checks on every store-scoped route.
7. Tamper-evident audit logging of security-relevant actions, 90d hot + 7y archived.
8. Daily encrypted, off-site (R2) database backups + local backups, 30-day retention;
   Neon point-in-time recovery, 7-day window; both restore paths drilled.
9. Automated dependency-vulnerability scanning, secret scanning, static analysis on
   every change; nightly dynamic (DAST) scanning of the production storefront.
10. Self-service data export and erasure endpoints (§10), reducing the window in which
    a manual/ad-hoc process could mishandle a data-subject request.

### Kuwait (retained detail — see also Annex 4)

- Kuwait's primary sector-specific instrument is CITRA's **Data Privacy Protection
  Regulation (DPPR)**, issued by the Communication and Information Technology Regulatory
  Authority under the general authority of Telecom Law No. 37 of 2014. Sources disagree
  on the exact current resolution/decision number and effective date — cited variously
  as Resolution No. 42 of 2021, Resolution No. 244 of 2023, and amended by
  Administrative Decision No. 26 of 2024. **Confirm the current in-force text directly
  with CITRA/counsel before citing a specific number in a signed document.**
  — [Kuwait Data Privacy Protection Regulation (DPPR) — Securiti](https://securiti.ai/kuwait-data-privacy-protection-regulation/)
  — [Data protection laws in Kuwait — DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/?t=law&c=KW)
  — [Data Protection & Privacy 2026 — Kuwait — Chambers and Partners](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/kuwait)
  — [Kuwait's DPPR Execution Approach — Ardent Privacy](https://www.ardentprivacy.ai/blog/kuwaits-data-privacy-protection-regulation-dppr-execution-approach/)
  — [CITRA publishes new Data Privacy Protection Regulation — DataGuidance](https://www.dataguidance.com/news/kuwait-citra-publishes-new-data-privacy-protection)
- **Scope is disputed between sources.** Securiti describes the DPPR as applying broadly
  to "all public and private sector service providers who conduct the collection,
  storage, and usage of personal data" in/from Kuwait; Chambers/DLA Piper describe it as
  applying "exclusively to CITRA-licensed telecom and internet providers." FWB is **not**
  a CITRA telecom/ISP licensee, so this scope question materially affects whether the
  DPPR binds FWB directly or only informs best practice — needs counsel to resolve.
- **Breach notification timeframe is also disputed**: 72 hours (2021 Resolution
  42/2021 text) per Securiti's reading vs. 24 hours (2024 Resolution/Decision 26/2024
  text, to CITRA + affected users, for licensees) per DLA Piper/Access Partnership's
  reading. This draft's proposed 24-hour SLA in §4.6 is a precautionary business choice
  that covers the stricter of the two disputed readings, not a claim about which one is
  the binding Kuwaiti number.
  — [Kuwait's DPPR: What Telecom and IT Licensees Must Do Within 24 Hours of a Breach — COMPLYAN](https://complyan.com/kuwaits-data-privacy-protection-regulations-what-telecom-and-it-licensees-must-do-within-24-hours-of-a-breach/)
  — [Access Partnership — Kuwaiti regulator introduces new data protection regulation](https://accesspartnership.com/opinion/access-alert-kuwaiti-regulator-introduces-new-data-protection-regulation/)
- **General (non-sectoral) data-subject rights** — right to access and to modify/delete
  personal data held by an entity — trace to Kuwait's **Electronic Transactions Law No.
  20 of 2014** (Articles 33 and 36), which is broader in application than the
  telecom-scoped DPPR and is the better general legal-basis citation for a non-CITRA-
  licensed platform like FWB. Not independently verified against the primary statute
  text in this pass.
  — [Data Protection & Privacy 2026 — Kuwait — Chambers and Partners](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/kuwait)
- **"Kuwait Law 13/2023" — could not be confirmed to exist.** Web search for this exact
  citation returned no matching Kuwaiti statute. The closest real match found is
  **Qatar's Law No. 13 of 2016 Concerning Personal Data Privacy Protection** — a
  different country's law with a similar number, which this draft flags as the likely
  source of the repo's existing (incorrect) internal shorthand. **Do not cite "Kuwait
  Law 13/2023" in any external-facing document until/unless a lawyer identifies a real
  statute matching that citation.**
  — [search: "Kuwait" "Law No. 13" 2023 personal data protection]
  — [Personal data protection legislation in the GCC jurisdiction — HFW](https://www.hfw.com/insights/personal-data-protection-legislation-in-the-gcc-jurisdiction/)
- **GDPR Art. 28** (controller/processor obligations) used here as the **structural
  reference model only**, per the task's instruction — relevant where FWB serves EU
  Shoppers or uses EU-hosted sub-processors (Neon's Frankfurt region, PostHog's EU
  region). None of the six GCC countries surveyed has a direct equivalent of GDPR as a
  single comprehensive statute, so Art. 28's processor-obligation structure (documented
  instructions, confidentiality, security, sub-processor authorisation, DSR assistance,
  breach notice, deletion/return, audit rights) was used to organise §4 of this draft,
  not presented as itself binding anywhere in the GCC.
  — [GDPR Article 28 — official text, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679)
